# THE LAST TRUTH

## A story of two atheists, the Day of Judgment, and the deepest depths of Jahannam

An Islamic-inspired work of fiction about two people who demanded proof, the reality they could not escape, and the moment every unanswered question finally received an answer.

Main characters

- Dr. Adam Rahman — a brilliant male physicist who believes that the universe needs no Creator.
- Dr. Layla Hassan — an uncompromising female neuroscientist who believes that consciousness ends when the brain dies.

Both are atheists. Both are intelligent. Both consider themselves honest seekers of truth. Neither believes that Allah exists, that the Quran is the word of God, or that a Day of Judgment awaits humanity.

They are about to discover a reality far beyond anything their instruments could measure.

Note: This is fictional storytelling inspired by Islamic beliefs about Allah, resurrection, judgment, and Jahannam. Its imagined conversations, characters, and scenes are not Quranic verses or authenticated hadith. The unseen details of the afterlife cannot be established through fiction.

# Prologue: The Two Who Feared Nothing

The rain had been falling over London for nearly six hours.

Beyond the enormous glass windows of the Halcyon Institute of Physics, the city glimmered beneath a blanket of water. Cars moved like streams of white and red light. People hurried beneath umbrellas, their faces lowered against the cold.

On the forty-second floor, however, two people were still arguing about God.

Dr. Adam Rahman stood beside a whiteboard covered in equations. He was thirty-four, tall, and almost unnervingly composed. His dark hair was beginning to recede at the temples, and his eyes possessed the permanent exhaustion of a man who slept too little and trusted numbers too much.

Across the room sat Dr. Layla Hassan, thirty-two, a neuroscientist whose research on consciousness had made her internationally respected. Her black hair was tied behind her head, and a half-empty cup of coffee rested beside a stack of research papers.

They had been colleagues for six years.

They had also been arguing for six years.

"You know what bothers me most about religion?" Adam asked.

Layla didn't look up from her laptop.

"That religious people keep asking you to stop talking?"

"Very funny."

"Then what?"

"People claim that an invisible being created everything, controls everything, knows everything, and deserves worship. Yet they tell us that the being's existence cannot be tested like any other claim."

Layla closed her laptop.

"Because people are uncomfortable with uncertainty. They invent explanations, give those explanations names, build institutions around them, and eventually call their beliefs truth."

Adam smiled.

"Exactly."

On the wall behind them hung a large photograph of a distant galaxy. Billions of stars burned in a structure so immense that the human mind could scarcely comprehend it.

Adam pointed toward it.

"Look at that. Hundreds of billions of galaxies, perhaps more than we can observe. Stars born and destroyed. Black holes. Radiation. Quantum fields. Physical laws. We can explain an extraordinary amount without inserting a supernatural being into our equations."

"That doesn't prove there is no Creator," Layla replied.

He turned toward her.

"Interesting. You're defending the religious position now?"

"I'm defending intellectual honesty. Not believing in God and proving that God does not exist are two different things."

Adam considered that.

"Fair enough."

Layla continued.

"But I don't believe in Allah either. I simply don't pretend that I have solved every question about existence."

Adam returned to the whiteboard.

"For me, the burden of proof remains with the person making the claim."

"And for me, evidence remains the only acceptable reason to change my mind."

They shook hands mockingly, as they had done countless times before.

Neither knew that within a few months, the words they had spoken would become the most painful memories of their existence.

Neither knew that one day they would stand before a reality that no laboratory could reproduce.

Neither knew that they would remember this conversation in a place where time, as they understood it, had lost all meaning.

And neither knew that their final argument about God would not take place in a university, a courtroom, or a scientific conference.

It would take place on the Day when every soul would stand before its Lord.

# Chapter One: The Experiment That Was Never Supposed to Exist

The discovery began with a mistake.

At least, that was what Adam called it.

The Halcyon Institute had constructed an experimental facility beneath the Swiss Alps. Its purpose was to investigate unusual gravitational fluctuations and the behavior of matter under extreme conditions.

The project was called the Horizon Experiment.

Its central machine was a circular arrangement of superconducting magnets surrounding a chamber designed to produce extraordinarily precise measurements of spacetime.

The project had cost hundreds of millions of pounds.

Adam was its theoretical director.

Layla had joined the project to investigate whether extreme physical conditions could affect neural tissue in ways that challenged existing models of consciousness.

Their work was controversial, but the preliminary results were promising.

Then, on a freezing Tuesday evening, something happened that no one could explain.

At 9:17 p.m., every clock in the underground facility stopped.

Not one after another.

All at once.

The digital displays froze. The analog clocks ceased moving. The computers registered an interruption lasting exactly 4.8 seconds, although their internal records contradicted one another about what had happened during that interval.

The magnetic field collapsed.

Every sensor recorded a spike beyond its expected operating range.

And inside the central chamber, a beam of light appeared.

It was not a laser.

It was not plasma.

It was not a reflection.

It resembled a narrow opening in the darkness, but there was nothing behind it that their instruments could measure.

Adam stared at the monitor.

"Run the calibration again."

The technician obeyed.

The result was identical.

"Again."

They repeated the experiment.

The same light appeared.

Layla approached the observation window.

The light seemed almost perfectly still. It had no visible source, produced no measurable heat, and cast no shadow.

Then something stranger happened.

For approximately one second, every person in the room heard a sound.

Not through the speakers.

Not through the walls.

Inside their own minds.

It was not a voice speaking a language. It was a sound like the memory of a voice, carrying a meaning that each person understood differently.

One technician began crying.

Another collapsed.

Adam stepped backward.

Layla gripped the edge of the console.

"What was that?" she whispered.

No one answered.

The light disappeared.

For the next forty-eight hours, the institute attempted to reproduce the event.

Nothing happened.

They examined the equipment, questioned every witness, reviewed every recording, and consulted specialists from several countries.

The official report described the incident as an unexplained instrument anomaly.

Adam rejected that explanation.

An anomaly was not an answer.

He spent three nights examining the raw data.

On the fourth night, he found something that made his hands go cold.

The experiment had registered information that did not appear to originate from any known input.

It was not a message, exactly.

It was a sequence of numbers, impossible to reconcile with the machine's configuration.

When he converted the sequence into a mathematical representation, he discovered that it corresponded to the precise coordinates of the facility.

Then he noticed a second pattern.

The numbers described the positions of the people who had been present in the room.

Every person.

Every movement.

Every heartbeat recorded by the medical sensors.

Even the position of a technician who had stood behind a wall.

Adam stared at the screen for nearly an hour.

Finally, he called Layla.

"You need to see this."

She arrived at his apartment before sunrise.

He showed her the data.

She examined it in silence.

"This could be a recording error," she said.

"It could."

"Or an interaction between the instruments."

"Possibly."

"Or a processing artifact."

"Yes."

She looked at him.

"Then why do you look as though you've seen a ghost?"

Adam closed the laptop.

"Because I have exhausted the ordinary explanations I know how to test."

Layla sat down.

For the first time in their professional relationship, neither had an argument ready.

They agreed to keep the discovery private until they could understand it.

They would spend the next several months trying to reproduce the event.

They would fail.

And then, in a way neither could have imagined, the experiment would end.

Not because they solved it.

Because their lives ended.

# Chapter Two: The Last Ordinary Day

The final day of Adam's life began with sunshine.

He woke late, drank coffee without breakfast, and checked the news on his phone.

There was a war in one country, a flood in another, and a political scandal dominating the headlines.

He read the reports with the detached concentration of a man who believed that human suffering was real but that the universe itself possessed no moral purpose.

He had never been cruel for the pleasure of cruelty.

He donated to scientific education, supported medical research, and had once spent months helping a refugee family obtain legal assistance.

He believed that people should be kind because suffering mattered to the person experiencing it.

He did not believe that morality required God.

Layla held similar views.

She volunteered at a neurological rehabilitation center. She had helped patients relearn how to speak, move, and recognize the faces of their families.

She had watched people suffer terrible losses without understanding why.

To her, the idea of an all-powerful God who permitted suffering seemed difficult to reconcile with the world she knew.

She had asked religious people about it many times.

Their answers had never persuaded her.

Some had told her that everything happened for a reason.

Others had told her that human beings could not understand divine wisdom.

She found those answers unsatisfying.

Adam and Layla respected each other's skepticism because neither considered the other stupid.

They considered themselves people who refused to believe merely because a belief was comforting.

At 6:40 p.m., Layla called Adam.

"The institute wants us at the facility tonight."

"Why?"

"They've detected another fluctuation."

He stood immediately.

"How large?"

"Large enough that they shut down the other experiments."

"Are the readings consistent?"

"Yes."

Adam looked through his apartment window.

The sky had darkened.

"Meet me there in an hour."

They arrived at the facility at 8:05 p.m.

The staff had already evacuated the upper laboratory. Only the essential research team remained underground.

The atmosphere was tense.

The machine was operating at a fraction of its normal power, yet the sensors showed a gravitational disturbance far beyond the range expected from the experiment.

Adam reviewed the readings.

"Shut it down."

The chief engineer hesitated.

"We haven't reached the instability threshold."

"Shut it down anyway."

The command was transmitted.

The machine began its shutdown sequence.

Then every screen went black.

A deep vibration traveled through the floor.

Layla grabbed the railing.

"Adam!"

The chamber's central ring began glowing.

Not with the blue-white light of the superconducting equipment.

With a color neither of them could describe.

The air became heavy.

Adam tried to speak, but his voice seemed distant even to himself.

The instruments displayed a single word.

ERROR.

Then the word disappeared.

The chamber filled with white light.

For a fraction of a second, Adam felt an extraordinary sensation: the absolute certainty that everything he had ever measured, studied, loved, rejected, or imagined existed within a reality far greater than his understanding.

He saw no face.

He heard no human voice.

He received no explanation.

He simply knew that his assumptions were incomplete.

Then came the pain.

It was sudden and overwhelming.

Layla reached toward him.

He tried to reach back.

Their fingers almost touched.

The world vanished.

# Chapter Three: The Silence Beyond Death

Adam expected nothing.

He had spent years telling students that consciousness depended on the brain. When the brain ceased functioning permanently, he believed, the person ceased to exist.

No dreams.

No darkness experienced by an observer.

No waiting.

No awareness.

Nothing.

But the first thing he discovered after death was that he was aware.

He could not explain how.

He could not identify a heartbeat.

He could not feel his lungs expanding.

He had no instrument, no body he could examine, and no familiar environment to orient himself.

Yet he was conscious.

And Layla was beside him.

"Adam?"

Her voice sounded distant.

"Layla?"

She turned toward him.

They appeared to stand in an immense darkness, although the word stand was no longer quite appropriate. They could perceive one another, but they could not understand what form their existence had taken.

Layla looked around.

"Where are we?"

"I don't know."

"Are we alive?"

Adam tried to examine himself.

"I don't think so."

She shook her head.

"No. This is impossible."

He wanted to agree.

He wanted to tell her that they were experiencing a hallucination, a dream, an unusual neurological event, or some unknown effect of the experiment.

But every explanation depended on assumptions he could no longer verify.

He could not inspect a brain.

He could not check a monitor.

He could not ask a technician for the time.

His scientific instruments were gone.

His familiar world was gone.

Yet the one thing he had believed impossible remained.

He was there.

Layla began to panic.

"This cannot be happening."

Adam reached toward her, but he did not know whether his hand could touch hers.

"Listen to me. We need to stay calm."

"Calm? You want me to stay calm?"

"I don't know what else to do."

For what seemed like an eternity, they waited.

Nothing appeared.

Nothing spoke.

No clock moved.

There was no wind, no floor, no sky.

They could not determine whether a second or a thousand years had passed.

Then, far away, something changed.

The darkness trembled.

A sound traveled through it.

It was not a voice.

It was not an explosion.

It resembled the beginning of a universe being awakened.

Adam turned toward the sound.

Layla followed his gaze.

A light appeared in the distance.

It spread across the darkness with an intensity that made every previous source of light seem insignificant.

And for the first time in their lives, neither of them asked whether the phenomenon could be measured.

They were too busy wondering what it meant.

# Chapter Four: The Day Promised to Every Soul

The light expanded.

The darkness disappeared.

What followed was beyond the reach of their previous understanding.

Adam found himself confronted with the reality of resurrection, a central teaching of Islam that he had once considered impossible.

Layla experienced the same overwhelming transformation.

The world they had known was no longer the world before them.

The Quran describes the resurrection as a day when humanity will be brought forth from its graves and gathered before Allah. In Islamic belief, death does not erase accountability, and the final judgment is not an imaginary courtroom constructed by human beings.

It is a reality appointed by the Creator.

Adam had spent years treating resurrection as a claim that could be dismissed because it exceeded his expectations about biology.

Now he understood that his expectations had never been the boundaries of existence.

He looked around.

Countless human beings were gathered in a vastness he could not comprehend.

People from different eras, nations, and generations stood together. The distinctions that had defined their lives—wealth, social standing, nationality, reputation, and professional achievement—could not protect anyone from the reality before them.

Adam saw people searching for their families.

He saw others overwhelmed by fear.

He saw individuals who had once commanded nations standing powerless.

And he saw people whose names had disappeared from every human record.

No one was forgotten.

No one could hide behind an institution, a government, a scientific theory, or the opinion of another person.

Layla stared at the gathering.

"How can this be real?"

Adam did not answer.

She turned toward him.

"Adam, say something."

"I can't."

"You have an explanation for everything."

"Not this."

She shook her head.

"No. There must be an explanation."

He looked at her.

"What if the explanation is that we were wrong about what reality permits?"

For once, Layla had no response.

Then the realization arrived, not as a mathematical deduction but as an unbearable recognition.

The God they had denied was not a hypothesis competing with another hypothesis.

They were standing in a reality in which their previous assumptions had failed.

The Quran had spoken of a Day when humanity would be resurrected.

They had dismissed the promise.

Now they stood within the event they had dismissed.

Adam remembered a passage he had once read during a debate about religion:

> "And that the Hour is coming—there is no doubt about it—and that Allah will resurrect those in the graves." — Quran 22:7, translated meaning

He had examined the verse as literature, history, and theology.

He had never imagined that he would remember it here.

Layla's expression changed.

"No," she whispered.

Adam looked at her.

"What?"

"If this is real..."

She could not finish.

Because the implication was larger than either of them could bear.

If Allah existed, then He was not merely another powerful being within the universe.

According to Islam, He was the Creator of the universe, independent of creation, possessing knowledge and power beyond human limitation.

If He had brought them back from death, then their previous understanding of death had not been complete.

If He had gathered every generation of humanity, then no human authority could overrule His judgment.

And if He had revealed guidance to humanity, then those revelations could no longer be treated as meaningless merely because they had rejected them.

Layla looked down.

"I thought I had more time."

Adam closed his eyes.

"So did I."

Neither could yet understand the judgment that awaited them.

But both understood that their old arguments had reached their end.

# Chapter Five: The Question They Had Never Answered

The gathering continued.

Adam could no longer measure the passage of time. He could not tell whether he had been waiting for minutes or for a duration beyond his imagination.

The fear surrounding him was unlike anything he had experienced in life.

He had been afraid before.

He had feared professional failure, disease, humiliation, and the death of people he loved.

Those fears had always existed alongside a future he could imagine.

Now there was no familiar future to which he could retreat.

Layla stood beside him.

"Do you remember our last conversation?" she asked.

"About the experiment?"

"Before that. About morality."

He remembered.

They had argued about whether moral responsibility required an ultimate judge.

Layla had maintained that human beings could act ethically without religion.

Adam had agreed.

Neither had claimed that atheists were inherently immoral. Neither had believed that religious people possessed an automatic monopoly on kindness.

Their disagreement had concerned something deeper.

Could human beings be the final authority on right and wrong?

Could a person commit an injustice, escape every human consequence, and still face no ultimate accountability?

They had believed that morality could be explained through human suffering, cooperation, empathy, and reason.

Now, standing before the Day of Judgment, they confronted the possibility that moral responsibility extended beyond the boundaries of human society.

Layla spoke quietly.

"I helped people. I tried to make their lives better."

"I know."

"I didn't believe in God, but I wasn't trying to hurt anyone."

"I know."

"Then why does everything feel as though I am standing before something I cannot defend myself against?"

Adam looked at the vast gathering.

"Because we are no longer judging ourselves."

She stared at him.

For years, they had relied on their own assessments of their lives.

They had remembered the good things they had done.

They had explained their mistakes.

They had judged religious claims according to their own standards of evidence.

But they had never possessed complete knowledge of themselves, much less of the universe.

Now they understood that Islamic judgment was not simply a matter of a person feeling guilty.

It was a judgment by Allah, whose knowledge, according to Islam, encompasses everything.

The Quran describes the Day of Resurrection as a day when even the smallest deeds will be brought to account.

Adam remembered another verse:

> "So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it." — Quran 99:7–8, translated meaning

He had once admired the ethical force of the passage without accepting its divine authority.

Now the words carried a weight he could not escape.

He remembered a junior researcher whose work he had presented as his own during a major conference.

He had told himself that he had contributed more to the project and that the decision was justified.

He remembered a technician he had dismissed after a dispute, despite knowing that the technician had raised legitimate safety concerns.

He remembered every time he had used his intelligence to win an argument rather than understand another person.

Layla remembered different things.

She remembered a patient who had asked whether death meant that he would never see his wife again.

She had told him that no reliable scientific evidence established the continuation of personal consciousness after death.

That had been her honest scientific assessment.

But she remembered the man's face afterward.

She remembered a colleague who had been humiliated during a presentation and whom she had failed to defend because she feared damaging her own reputation.

She remembered the elderly woman whose religious beliefs she had mocked during an argument, even though the woman had done nothing to deserve her contempt.

Neither Adam nor Layla could determine which memories would matter most in the judgment ahead.

They were not entitled to declare themselves innocent.

Nor could they assume that every good deed they had done was meaningless.

They could only recognize the difference between defending themselves and knowing the complete truth.

Layla began to cry.

"I thought being honest meant admitting what I could prove."

Adam replied, "Perhaps honesty also requires admitting what we don't know."

She looked at him.

"And we were so certain."

He had no answer.

# Chapter Six: When the Record Was Opened

In Islamic belief, the deeds of human beings are recorded, and on the Day of Judgment, people will confront the truth of their lives.

The Quran describes the record being placed before humanity and the astonishment of those who find that it has omitted nothing of significance.

Adam and Layla now stood in the presence of that reckoning.

They could no longer rely on selective memories.

They could not erase their mistakes by changing the story.

They could not turn an unjust action into a just one merely by providing a clever explanation.

The truth of their actions was no longer limited to what they remembered or what other people knew.

Adam felt a terrible urge to speak.

"I didn't know."

The words escaped him before he could stop them.

He looked around, as though searching for someone who might understand.

"I didn't know that this was real."

Layla gripped his arm.

"I didn't know either."

But neither statement answered every question.

In Islamic teaching, Allah is perfectly just. People are not simply judged according to an outsider's superficial impression of their lives. Questions of knowledge, intention, access to guidance, responsibility, and the circumstances in which people lived belong to Allah's judgment.

Their fictional story could not determine the precise judgment that would be made in every real human case.

But in the world of this story, Adam and Layla had both spent years deliberately dismissing the possibility that they might owe their existence to a Creator.

They had encountered religious arguments.

They had read portions of the Quran.

They had spoken to believers.

They had decided that no claim about Allah deserved their belief.

They had treated their conclusions as final.

Now they stood before the reality they had rejected.

Adam remembered how confidently he had spoken.

"Religion is an explanation invented by people who are afraid of death."

He had said those words during a televised debate.

The audience had applauded.

He had smiled.

He had felt victorious.

Now the memory returned with devastating clarity.

Layla remembered her own words from an interview.

"If someone claims that an all-powerful God exists, I will believe it when the evidence justifies belief."

She had considered that statement reasonable.

And the story now confronted her with the reality she had refused to accept.

Yet her fear was not simply the fear of being wrong.

It was the fear of standing before the One whose existence, power, and knowledge made every human judgment incomplete.

She whispered, "I wish I had taken the question more seriously."

Adam looked at her.

"So do I."

They were no longer debating the existence of Allah.

They were confronting the consequences of the lives they had lived.

# Chapter Seven: The Reality of Jahannam

The Quran speaks of Jannah, Paradise, and Jahannam, Hell, as realities of the Hereafter.

Islam teaches that Allah is the Lord of the worlds and that the final judgment belongs to Him alone.

The Quran also describes the fire of Hell as a punishment of terrifying severity. It speaks of boiling water, chains, the suffering of its inhabitants, and their desperate pleas for relief.

Among the most sobering passages are those that describe people asking for an escape from the Fire, only to find that the consequences of their earthly lives cannot be undone by simply demanding another chance.

Adam and Layla had reached the moment when the story of their lives would no longer be measured by professional achievement, intellectual prestige, or public reputation.

They faced judgment.

Adam could not see every detail of what was happening around him.

He could not understand the order of events.

He knew only that the reality before him was not an illusion he could dismiss.

Then came the moment he had feared without understanding why.

The judgment in this fictional narrative ended with Adam and Layla condemned to Jahannam.

They had not been granted the outcome they had once assumed did not exist.

They had not escaped accountability through death.

They had not been protected by the fact that they had possessed intelligence or that they had helped other people during their lives.

Their good actions and wrong actions belonged to the complete truth of their lives, and the final judgment belonged to Allah.

They saw the Fire.

It was not like an ordinary blaze.

There was no comforting distance between the observer and the danger.

The sight overwhelmed them.

Adam tried to look away.

Layla covered her face.

Neither could find words for what they were witnessing.

The Quran describes Hell as a place whose fire is unlike the familiar fires of the world, and Islamic tradition contains warnings intended to make people take the Hereafter seriously.

Adam had once treated those warnings as religious imagery.

Now he could no longer reassure himself that the reality of the Hereafter was merely a human invention.

He looked at Layla.

"We were wrong."

She could barely speak.

"Yes."

He shook his head.

"No. I mean completely wrong about what we thought we knew."

Layla stared toward the Fire.

"I wanted evidence."

"And now?"

She turned toward him, tears in her eyes.

"Now I wish I had understood what evidence could mean when the reality itself was greater than my expectations."

A terrible realization settled between them.

They had spent their lives discussing whether Allah existed.

They had spent almost no time considering what they would do if the answer were yes.

# Chapter Eight: The Gate of the Fire

In the Quran, the inhabitants of Hell are described as being brought to the Fire, while its keepers address them with questions about the warnings and messengers that had come to them.

The Quran also speaks of the gates of Jahannam and of its inhabitants entering it according to Allah's judgment.

The following scene imagines that moment for Adam and Layla. It is a fictional scene, not a claim that the Quran or authentic hadith describes these two people or their precise experience.

The gate stood before them.

Adam could not understand its dimensions.

It seemed to stretch beyond the boundaries of anything he could measure. Its darkness was more terrible than ordinary darkness, and the heat that came from beyond it overwhelmed every memory he possessed of summer, fire, or industrial furnaces.

Layla trembled.

"Adam, please tell me this is not happening."

He wanted to tell her.

He wanted to give her one final explanation that would restore the world they had lost.

But he had none.

Beyond the gate, they could hear sounds of anguish.

They could not identify every voice.

They could not understand every cry.

Yet the meaning was unmistakable.

These were people suffering within the reality that they had denied.

Adam remembered a Quranic passage describing the keepers of Hell asking its inhabitants whether messengers had come to warn them.

He had once studied that passage for a lecture on religious ideas of punishment.

Now he understood the difference between analyzing a warning and confronting the reality to which it pointed.

The gate opened.

The heat seemed to strike every thought from his mind.

Layla screamed.

Adam tried to hold on to her.

They crossed the threshold.

And the world of their earthly certainty ended forever.

# Chapter Nine: The Lowest Depths

Adam had once studied the deepest ocean trenches.

He had read about the crushing pressure of the Mariana Trench, the darkness where sunlight never reaches, and the extraordinary organisms that survive in conditions that would destroy a human being.

He had also studied black holes, where gravity can bend the structure of spacetime beyond the limits of ordinary intuition.

He had considered these places terrifying precisely because they demonstrated how small human beings were in comparison with the physical universe.

But nothing he had imagined prepared him for Jahannam.

In the fictional world of this story, Adam and Layla were assigned to its lowest depths.

Islamic sources speak of different levels or degrees of Hell, and Quran 4:145 describes the hypocrites as being in the lowest depths of the Fire. However, the Quran does not identify every atheist as automatically occupying that particular level, and it would be incorrect to claim that authentic Islamic texts specify the exact destination of these two fictional characters.

Here, the lowest depths are part of the imagined narrative.

They descended.

The word descended did not fully describe the experience.

There was no familiar staircase.

No measurable distance.

No instrument by which Adam could determine how far they had traveled.

There was only the overwhelming sense of being taken farther and farther away from everything that had once made existence bearable.

At first, Adam tried to count.

One.

Two.

Three.

He had counted seconds during experiments, calculated the age of stars, and measured intervals so small that they could not be perceived without sophisticated instruments.

Counting had always given him a sense of control.

But here, the numbers brought no comfort.

He lost his count.

He began again.

Then the pain overwhelmed his concentration.

He cried out.

He could not understand how consciousness could continue under such suffering.

He could not understand how he could remember his earthly life with such clarity.

He could not understand why the mind that had once solved complex equations was now incapable of answering the simplest question.

How do I escape?

Layla was beside him.

She had stopped trying to explain what was happening.

Her scientific vocabulary had not disappeared. She still knew the definitions of heat, pain, neurological damage, and consciousness.

But those definitions offered no protection.

She remembered patients who had suffered catastrophic injuries.

She remembered how carefully she had explained the limits of medicine.

She remembered telling families that some forms of damage could not be reversed.

Now she confronted a reality in which the language of medicine could not provide relief.

"Adam!" she screamed.

He heard her.

He tried to answer.

The pain interrupted him.

They were no longer the respected scientists who had stood before audiences and defended their views.

They were two human beings who had discovered that their knowledge had not made them masters of existence.

They had reached the depths of Jahannam in this imagined account, and neither could find a way to escape.

# Chapter Ten: The Fire That No Human Imagination Could Measure

The Quran describes the fire of Jahannam as a punishment of immense severity.

It describes boiling water, burning, and the desperate suffering of those who rejected the truth and persisted in wrongdoing.

Some Quranic passages describe the inhabitants of Hell being given boiling water to drink, which cuts through their insides. Other passages describe their skin being replaced so that they continue to experience punishment.

These descriptions are warnings within Islamic scripture. The precise experience of the unseen should not be expanded into invented details and then falsely presented as established religious doctrine.

The following scene imagines Adam and Layla's suffering in the lowest depths of Jahannam.

Adam had once believed that every physical phenomenon, however extraordinary, must ultimately be governed by laws that could be investigated.

Now he found himself in a reality in which no familiar measurement offered him an answer.

The suffering seemed to consume every moment of his awareness.

He tried to focus on memories.

He remembered his mother's voice.

He remembered the smell of rain on the pavement outside his childhood home.

He remembered his first laboratory, with its unreliable equipment and flickering fluorescent lights.

He remembered the day he had received his doctorate.

He remembered the first time he had met Layla.

He clung to these memories because they were the only remnants of a world in which life had contained moments of comfort.

But the memories could not stop the suffering.

They only reminded him of what he had lost.

Layla remembered her father teaching her mathematics at the kitchen table.

She remembered the excitement of her first discovery.

She remembered her patients, especially the ones who had recovered enough to recognize their families.

She remembered laughing with Adam after an argument that had lasted nearly an entire evening.

She had once thought that human consciousness was a fragile biological process that ended when the brain ceased to function.

Now she understood that the assumptions she had made about the limits of consciousness were not sufficient to explain the reality she was experiencing.

"Why?" she cried.

The word escaped her repeatedly.

"Why did I refuse to believe?"

Adam could not answer.

She turned toward him.

"We were not stupid."

"No."

"We weren't trying to be evil."

"No."

"Then how did we end up here?"

Adam closed his eyes.

"Because being intelligent isn't the same as being right."

Layla shook her head.

"I thought I was following the evidence."

"Perhaps we should have asked whether we were willing to follow it wherever it led."

She stared at him.

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then Layla whispered something she had not said in her entire earthly life.

"Allah, if You hear me, I understand now."

Adam looked at her.

He had no way to know what her words meant in the judgment of the Hereafter. He could not tell her whether her plea would change anything.

He could only recognize that the woman who had once dismissed the possibility of God was now addressing the Creator she had denied.

He, too, wanted to speak.

He wanted to acknowledge every mistake.

He wanted to ask for another chance.

But the opportunity to make a new earthly life had passed.

The Quran repeatedly warns humanity not to wait until the consequences of its choices become undeniable before turning toward Allah.

Adam had once considered that warning emotionally persuasive but intellectually unconvincing.

Now it seemed to describe the very danger he had ignored.

# Chapter Eleven: The Endless Questions

In the depths of Jahannam, the two scientists tried to reconstruct their lives.

They did not have laboratory instruments.

They did not have books.

They did not have access to the internet.

They could not summon a professor, a cleric, or a philosopher to settle their arguments.

All they had were their memories and the terrible clarity of their situation.

Layla spoke first.

"Do you remember the debate at Oxford?"

Adam nodded.

"You said religious belief was a psychological response to uncertainty."

"I remember."

"You told me I was confusing the need for comfort with the existence of truth."

"I remember that too."

She looked away.

"I think I was afraid of being deceived."

Adam listened.

"I thought that if I accepted religion without enough evidence, I would be surrendering my independence. I didn't want to believe something merely because my family believed it, or because I had been taught it as a child."

"That wasn't an unreasonable concern," Adam replied.

She looked at him in surprise.

"You're defending me?"

"I'm saying that questioning claims isn't the same as rejecting every possible answer before examining it."

Layla remained silent.

Adam continued.

"We both thought that the only way to protect ourselves from false beliefs was to refuse belief until we could eliminate every doubt."

"And you think that was our mistake?"

"I think we treated our own standards as though they were infallible."

He remembered the arrogance with which he had dismissed religious scholars who had spent their lives studying theology.

He remembered how often he had assumed that a believer could not possibly have examined the arguments as carefully as he had.

He remembered the satisfaction he felt when he defeated someone in public.

He had considered intellectual victory evidence of intellectual superiority.

Now he understood that winning an argument and discovering the truth were not the same thing.

Layla remembered something else.

A Muslim student had once asked her whether she had read the Quran in a language she understood.

She had answered that she had read enough to know that she did not believe it.

The student had asked whether she had studied its historical context, its theological arguments, and the strongest explanations offered by Muslim scholars.

She had told him that she did not have time to investigate every religious tradition in the world.

At the time, she had considered the answer reasonable.

Now she wondered whether she had confused a legitimate limit on her time with a complete examination of the question.

"Adam," she said, "do you think everyone who disbelieves is punished like us?"

He looked at her.

"I don't know."

"But Islam says Allah is just."

"Yes."

"Then how can we know that our judgment represents everyone else's?"

"We can't."

For the first time since their condemnation in this fictional account, Layla seemed to recognize a distinction that mattered.

Their imagined fate was not a license for them to declare the fate of every atheist, every agnostic, every person who had never heard a convincing presentation of Islam, or every person who had struggled with religious questions.

Allah's knowledge was not theirs.

His judgment was not theirs.

They could not transform their personal experience into a universal ruling.

They could only confront the truth of their own fictional lives.

Layla lowered her head.

"I used to think uncertainty meant I should withhold judgment."

"And now?"

"Now I think uncertainty should have made me more careful."

Adam looked toward the darkness.

"I wish we had understood that sooner."

# Chapter Twelve: The Water They Could Not Find

The Quran describes the inhabitants of Hell calling upon the people of Paradise, asking them to pour water upon them or provide something of the provision Allah has granted them.

The answer they receive is that Allah has forbidden these things to those who rejected the truth.

This passage appears in Quran 7:50.

It is among the most sobering descriptions of the Hereafter because it portrays a moment when a simple necessity becomes the subject of a desperate plea.

The following scene is fictional, inspired by that Quranic image.

Layla could no longer remember the last time she had thought about water.

In life, water had been ordinary.

She had drunk it while working at her desk, carried a bottle into the laboratory, and poured glasses for guests in her apartment.

She had never considered the existence of drinking water a miracle.

It was a basic condition of biological life.

Now the memory of water became almost unbearable.

She remembered cold water from a mountain stream during a university expedition.

She remembered the first glass she had drunk after a long day in the laboratory.

She remembered a glass of water her mother had brought her during a fever.

Small memories.

Ordinary memories.

Things she had never thought to be grateful for.

She turned toward Adam.

"Do you remember the lake near the university?"

He nodded weakly.

"We used to sit there after work."

"You complained that the water was contaminated."

"I was probably right."

Despite everything, a faint, broken laugh escaped her.

Then she began to cry.

"I would give anything to sit there again."

Adam remembered the lake.

He remembered sunlight on the surface, the sound of birds, and the cool air moving through the trees.

He remembered how he had once watched a religious student pray beside the water and had wondered how anyone could believe that an invisible Creator was listening.

He had believed that the natural world was enough.

Now the memory of the lake was a reminder that the world he had taken for granted was gone.

Layla whispered, "I never thanked Allah for anything."

Adam looked at her.

"We didn't believe we had anyone to thank."

She closed her eyes.

"That was the point."

The memory of water did not relieve their suffering.

It only reminded them of the countless ordinary gifts that they had once possessed without understanding their significance.

# Chapter Thirteen: The Truth About Omnipotence

Adam had spent his career studying the limits of physical possibility.

He had investigated energy, matter, and spacetime.

He had written papers about what the laws of nature permitted and what they appeared to forbid.

He had often told students that a scientifically meaningful explanation must distinguish between what is impossible, what is improbable, and what has not yet been observed.

Now he confronted a question he had never truly resolved.

What did it mean for a being to be omnipotent?

He had once imagined that an all-powerful God would be an enormously powerful object within the universe, capable of producing extraordinary events.

But Islamic theology describes Allah differently.

Allah is not a physical object contained within creation.

He does not depend on the universe for His existence.

He is the Creator, while the universe and everything within it depend upon Him.

His knowledge is not limited by the instruments of human observation.

His power is not measured by the energy available to a laboratory.

His existence is not established by inserting Him into an equation as one more physical cause among others.

Adam understood, at least conceptually, why Islamic theology rejected the idea that Allah must be constrained by the physical laws governing created things.

If the universe itself was created by Allah, then the universe could not be the ultimate authority over Him.

He remembered a verse from the Quran:

> "Indeed, Allah is over all things competent." — Quran 2:20, translated meaning

He had once read those words as a declaration of religious belief.

Now, within the fictional world of this story, he understood them as a statement about the relationship between Creator and creation.

The experiment that had destroyed their ordinary lives had not demonstrated that Allah was a mysterious energy, a hidden dimension, or an undiscovered physical force.

It had shattered their assumption that physical explanations, as they understood them, exhausted the possibilities of reality.

Layla spoke.

"Do you think we could ever have understood Him completely?"

Adam shook his head.

"No. Not completely."

"Then what should we have done?"

"Perhaps we should have stopped demanding that the Creator fit inside the limits of our understanding before we were willing to consider Him."

Layla looked at him.

"That sounds different from abandoning reason."

"It is."

He paused.

"Reason can tell us when an argument is weak. It can help us examine evidence. It can expose contradictions. But reason doesn't make us omniscient."

For Adam, that was the final collapse of a belief he had never recognized as a belief.

He had not merely doubted Allah.

He had trusted his own judgment as though it were sufficient to settle every question.

Now he understood the difference between possessing intelligence and possessing complete knowledge.

The difference was immeasurable.

# Chapter Fourteen: The Second Chance That Never Came

The most painful part of their imagined punishment was not a new discovery about science.

It was the memory of time.

Adam remembered the years he had spent debating religion.

He remembered the books he had read, the arguments he had rejected, and the conversations he had ended before they could become uncomfortable.

He remembered the occasions when a religious person had admitted uncertainty and he had interpreted that honesty as weakness.

He remembered the occasions when a believer had made a poor argument and he had treated that failure as proof that the entire faith was false.

He remembered how often he had said, "I will believe when I see."

Now he had seen the reality he had denied.

And he could not return to the moment when he had spoken those words.

Layla remembered her own decisions.

She had never wanted to worship an imaginary being.

She had feared intellectual dishonesty more than she had feared death.

She had believed that withholding belief was the safest course until she had sufficient evidence.

But she had also allowed her skepticism to become a shield.

She had stopped listening when she expected a religious answer.

She had dismissed arguments before understanding them fully.

She had treated the possibility of being wrong as a theoretical inconvenience rather than a question that deserved serious examination.

"Adam," she whispered, "if we could return to the beginning, would you believe?"

He did not answer immediately.

"I would investigate more carefully."

"Would you pray?"

"I don't know what I would have believed then."

She looked at him.

"At least you're honest."

He nodded.

"I can't change the past by pretending I know exactly what I would have done."

She began to cry again.

"I want one ordinary morning."

He listened.

"One morning when I wake up and the world is still there. I want to drink coffee. I want to read the news. I want to go to work and know that I have another day to think."

Adam could not offer her that morning.

Neither could anyone else.

In Islamic teaching, the Hereafter is not a continuation of earthly life in which people can simply repeat the same choices indefinitely. The Quran describes people who plead to return to the world and act differently, only to be denied the return they request.

Their regret is one of the central warnings of the Day of Judgment.

Adam remembered the meaning of that warning.

Layla remembered it too.

They had once treated time as an unlimited resource.

They had assumed that if a question mattered, they could always return to it later.

But no human being knows the length of their life.

No one can guarantee that the next conversation, the next morning, or the next opportunity will arrive.

Their tragedy, in this story, was not that they had lacked intelligence.

It was that they had mistaken the availability of another day for a certainty.

# Chapter Fifteen: The Last Argument

After what seemed to them an immeasurable duration, Adam and Layla spoke again.

They no longer argued as they had in the laboratory.

There were no equations between them.

No audience.

No microphones.

No reputation to protect.

Layla asked the question that had haunted her since the beginning.

"Do you think we deserved this?"

Adam was silent.

The question was more difficult than any he had answered before.

He could remember the good they had done.

He could remember their mistakes.

He could remember the limits of their knowledge and the moments when they had chosen not to examine a question more carefully.

But he was not Allah.

He could not claim complete knowledge of divine justice.

He could not determine the fate of every person who had ever rejected religion.

And he could not use his own imagined experience to establish the exact judgment that would apply to anyone else.

"In this story," he said slowly, "we were judged by Allah, and the judgment was not something we could overturn."

Layla looked at him.

"That isn't quite what I asked."

"I know."

He paused.

"I think the question we should have asked while we were alive was whether we were prepared to be wrong about something that mattered eternally."

She lowered her head.

"I thought I was prepared."

"So did I."

"Then why weren't we?"

Adam had no answer.

For the first time, Layla understood that an argument could end without either person possessing a satisfying explanation.

The reality of their fictional punishment did not make them omniscient.

It did not give them access to every secret of the universe.

It did not allow them to judge other people.

It did not turn their suffering into a source of authority over anyone else.

It only confronted them with the consequences of the lives they had lived in the story.

Layla whispered, "I wanted to know the truth."

Adam replied, "So did I."

"And now?"

He looked into the darkness.

"Now we know that wanting the truth and being willing to accept it are not always the same thing."

They fell silent.

Their final argument was not about whether Allah existed.

That question had ended for them.

Their final argument was about the lives they had spent before the answer became undeniable.

# Chapter Sixteen: What the Living Must Understand

This story ends in Jahannam because that is the fate imagined for its two central characters.

But the lesson should not be that every atheist is automatically destined for the same fate.

Islamic teaching places ultimate judgment in Allah's hands. Human beings do not possess His complete knowledge of another person's understanding, intentions, circumstances, or access to guidance.

Nor should the story be read as proof of Islam through a fictional experiment. A story can illustrate a belief, explore its consequences, and make its warnings emotionally vivid. It cannot, by itself, establish that a supernatural event occurred.

The Islamic message is more serious than that.

The Quran calls people to reflect upon creation, examine the signs around them, consider revelation, and recognize that worldly life is temporary.

It warns against arrogance.

It warns against injustice.

It warns against assuming that wealth, intelligence, influence, or scientific achievement makes a person independent of the Creator.

And it warns people not to postpone questions of ultimate importance until the opportunity to act has passed.

Adam and Layla had possessed extraordinary intelligence.

They had investigated the universe.

They had helped people.

They had questioned religious claims.

But in this fictional account, they had also become so confident in their conclusions that they failed to take the possibility of being wrong seriously enough.

Their story is not an instruction to abandon reason.

It is a warning against treating one's own reason as infallible.

It is not an instruction to hate those who disagree.

It is a reminder that disagreement should be approached with honesty, patience, and humility.

And it is not a declaration that human beings can determine the eternal fate of everyone around them.

It is a story about two people who discovered, too late in their imagined lives, that the truth they had rejected was greater than the world they had understood.

# Epilogue: The Morning That Never Returned

Somewhere in the world they had left behind, rain was falling over London again.

The Halcyon Institute had been closed following the accident.

An official investigation had begun.

News organizations reported that two leading researchers had died during an unexplained laboratory incident. Experts debated the equipment failure, the possibility of a structural defect, and the strange measurements recorded during the final seconds.

Adam's apartment remained locked.

Layla's books sat on her shelves.

Their unfinished papers remained on their computers.

A coffee cup stood on a desk in the laboratory, untouched.

The world continued.

People went to work.

Children went to school.

Families prepared meals.

Scientists conducted experiments.

Believers prayed.

Atheists debated.

Some people faced death without fear.

Others were terrified of what might follow.

And countless people continued asking the oldest questions humanity had ever known.

Who created the universe?

Why does anything exist?

What is consciousness?

Does death end everything?

Is there a Creator?

Can human beings know the ultimate truth?

No fictional story can answer every one of those questions merely by imagining a dramatic ending.

But the Islamic message at the heart of this story is clear: Allah is not presented in Islam as a created being competing with other beings. He is the One, the Creator, the Sustainer, and the ultimate authority over life, death, resurrection, and judgment.

The Quran describes Him as possessing complete knowledge and power, and it repeatedly calls human beings to take their lives, their choices, and their accountability seriously.

Adam and Layla had once believed that death would erase every unanswered question.

In this story, death did not erase the questions.

It ended their opportunity to answer them through the choices of earthly life.

And in the lowest depths of Jahannam, where neither scientific prestige nor intellectual confidence could protect them, they remembered the ordinary morning they had once believed was guaranteed.

The rain.

The coffee.

The laboratory.

The conversations.

The possibility of changing their minds.

The possibility of asking another question.

The possibility of seeking the truth while the opportunity remained.

And the morning that never returned.

The End.

# The Last Light in the Universe

An original science-fiction mystery story.

At 2:17 AM, the universe went silent.

Not quiet. Silent.

Every star stopped shining. Every planet lost its atmosphere. Every radio signal, every heartbeat, every sound that had ever existed disappeared in a single instant.

Except for one small room on Earth.

Inside that room, a twenty-three-year-old man named Arin sat in front of an old computer, watching a message appear on his screen.

UNKNOWN TRANSMISSION RECEIVED.

Arin had spent years collecting things people had forgotten: old photographs, abandoned websites, forgotten music, broken games, and stories nobody remembered telling. He believed that if something was preserved somewhere, it never truly disappeared.

But the message on his screen was different.

It contained only seven words.

You are the last memory of everything.

Arin stared at the sentence.

Then another message appeared.

Do not look outside.

Naturally, he looked outside.

Beyond his window, the city was frozen beneath a black sky. Buildings stood like lifeless monuments. Cars had stopped in the middle of the roads. Even the moon had vanished.

And where the stars should have been, there was an enormous eye.

It was watching him.

Arin stepped away from the window.

His computer chimed.

We have found you.

"Who are you?" he whispered.

The reply appeared immediately.

We are what remains after the end.

Arin typed with trembling fingers.

"What happened to the universe?"

For several seconds, nothing happened.

Then the screen filled with thousands of photographs.

Galaxies. Civilizations. Oceans. Forests. Children playing beneath unfamiliar suns. Creatures that had never existed on Earth. Entire worlds that humanity had never discovered.

Every image faded to black.

One by one.

Finally, a sentence appeared.

The universe has reached its final second.

Arin looked at the clock.

2:18 AM.

The second hand was not moving.

He typed, "Why am I still alive?"

The answer was short.

Because you remembered us.

Suddenly, he understood something impossible.

The universe was not a place.

It was a memory.

Every star existed because something had once observed its light. Every civilization existed because something had once known its name. Every life left a tiny mark on reality, and those marks held the universe together.

But time had reached its end.

The memories were disappearing.

And Arin, a man who had spent his life preserving forgotten things, had somehow become the final keeper of existence.

His computer displayed a new option.

RESTORE UNIVERSE?

Below it were two choices.

YES.

NO.

Arin moved the cursor toward YES.

A warning appeared.

Restoration requires one living memory as its foundation.

He stopped.

"What does that mean?"

The reply came slowly.

If you restore everything, you will disappear.

Arin looked around his little room.

His old phone lay beside the keyboard. His photographs were stacked on a shelf. His favorite songs waited inside a folder on his hard drive. Every ordinary thing suddenly felt precious.

He had spent his life afraid of losing things.

Now he was being asked to choose between preserving everything and preserving himself.

He thought about the people he had never met, the children who would never be born, the oceans that would never rise beneath another sunrise.

He thought about all the stories that had not yet been told.

Then he smiled.

"One memory," he said. "That's all you need?"

The computer replied:

One memory that refuses to forget.

Arin clicked YES.

Light erupted from the screen.

For a moment, he saw everything.

The first star igniting in the darkness. The first ocean forming on a newborn planet. The first living creature opening its eyes. The first human looking up at the night sky and wondering whether anyone else existed.

He saw civilizations rise and fall across a billion worlds.

He saw the last star die.

And he saw himself, sitting alone in a dark room, choosing to remember it all.

Then Arin disappeared.

The universe breathed again.

Stars returned to the sky. The moon appeared above the city. Traffic moved. Somewhere, a baby cried. Millions of people woke from strange dreams they would never fully understand.

At 2:19 AM, a man in another country switched on his computer.

A new file had appeared on his desktop.

Its name was THE_LAST_MEMORY.txt.

Inside was a single sentence.

If you can remember something that no longer exists, then perhaps it has never truly died.

The man read it twice.

Then he saved the file.

Far beyond the edges of the observable universe, in a place where time had not yet learned how to begin, a tiny light appeared.

It had no planet.

No star.

No observer.

Yet it shone.

And somewhere inside that light, a voice whispered:

"Let there be something."

The universe began again.

But this time, it remembered that it had once ended.

And it would never be quite the same.

The End.
